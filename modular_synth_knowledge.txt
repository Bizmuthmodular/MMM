# Modular Synthesizer Knowledge Base

This file provides foundational knowledge about modular synthesizer concepts, signal types, and module behavior to inform AI-assisted development work on Eurorack modules, Disting NT algorithms, and related audio DSP projects.

---

## 1. Signal Types in Eurorack

All signals in a Eurorack system are analog voltages. The same physical jack and cable type is used for everything — the distinction between signal types is purely conceptual and contextual.

### Audio Signals
- Oscillate in the audible frequency range (roughly 20 Hz – 20 kHz)
- Typically ±5V peak-to-peak (10 Vpp)
- The Eurorack power supply provides ±12V rails; no signal should exceed this range
- Audio signals can be used as CV (for FM, AM, etc.) — the distinction is about frequency and intent, not voltage format

### CV (Control Voltage)
- A continuous analog voltage used to control a parameter on another module
- Unipolar CV: typically 0–10V or 0–8V (e.g., envelope outputs)
- Bipolar CV: typically ±5V (e.g., LFO outputs, modulation sources)
- Common CV targets: oscillator pitch, filter cutoff, VCA gain, panning, effect parameters

### Pitch CV (1V/oct)
- The standard for pitch control in Eurorack (established by Bob Moog)
- Each 1V increase raises pitch by exactly one octave
- The 12 semitones of an octave are linearly distributed within 1V (1 semitone = ~83.3 mV)
- Example: 0V = some base pitch (often C), 1V = one octave up, 2V = two octaves up
- Most oscillators follow this standard; always check — older Korg/Yamaha gear uses Hz/V, which is incompatible without conversion
- VCV Rack standard: C4 (middle C, 261.6 Hz) at 0V

### Gate Signals
- A high/low voltage that remains high for the duration of a note
- "High" is typically +5V or higher (Eurorack standard treats anything above ~1V as high)
- Goes high when a key is pressed (or a sequencer step is active), goes low when released
- Used to trigger envelope generators and indicate note duration
- A gate held high keeps an envelope in its sustain phase

### Trigger Signals
- A brief pulse, typically only a few milliseconds long
- Unlike a gate, it doesn't carry duration information — just "now"
- Used to: retrigger envelopes, reset sequencers/LFOs, fire one-shot events, clock sequencers
- Retriggering an envelope mid-note snaps it back to the attack phase, creating percussive effects

### Clock Signals
- A regular stream of triggers or gates at a defined tempo (BPM)
- Square wave oscillating at sub-audio rates
- Dividers and multipliers can derive different rhythmic subdivisions from a master clock

### S-Trig (Short-Circuit Trigger)
- Legacy format used in early Moog modular systems
- Normally held high; shorts to ground to trigger
- Incompatible with modern V-trig without conversion

## 2. The Basic Synthesizer Signal Chain

The classic "East Coast" (Moog-style) subtractive synthesis voice:

```
[VCO] → [VCF] → [VCA] → [output]
          ↑        ↑
       [EG2]    [EG1]
          ↑        ↑
       [Gate] ← [Gate]
       
[Sequencer/Keyboard] → Pitch CV → [VCO]
[Sequencer/Keyboard] → Gate → [EG1, EG2]
```

- **VCO**: generates the raw waveform at the pitch defined by incoming CV
- **VCF**: shapes timbre by filtering harmonics
- **VCA**: controls amplitude — keeps the oscillator from droning continuously
- **EG (envelope generator)**: shapes how VCF and VCA change over time in response to a gate
- **Sequencer / keyboard**: provides pitch CV and gate signals

**West Coast (Buchla-style)** synthesis uses a different philosophy: complex oscillators (often using FM or waveshaping) fed through a Low Pass Gate (LPG), which combines VCA and filter behavior in a single element controlled by a vactrol. Shorter, punchier, more percussive and harmonically rich — less reliance on filtering, more on oscillator complexity.

## 3. VCO — Voltage Controlled Oscillator

The primary sound source. Oscillators run continuously (they never stop) — the VCA is what creates silence between notes.

### Waveform Types
- **Sine**: smooth, no harmonics beyond the fundamental. Soft, pure tone.
- **Triangle**: only odd harmonics, attenuating quickly. Sonically similar to sine but slightly brighter.
- **Sawtooth (ramp)**: contains all harmonics (odd and even). Bright and buzzy — the classic subtractive synthesis source.
- **Square / pulse**: only odd harmonics. Hollow, "woodwind"-like sound. Pulse width determines timbre (PWM = pulse width modulation, a common dynamic effect).
- **Noise**: all frequencies simultaneously. Used for percussion, wind, and random modulation sources.

### VCO Inputs
- **1V/oct CV**: sets pitch
- **FM (linear or exponential)**: modulates frequency — audio-rate FM creates sidebands and complex timbres; LFO-rate FM creates vibrato
- **Sync**: hard sync resets the waveform cycle to the phase of an incoming signal, creating aggressive harmonic effects

### Oscillator Modes
- **Audio-rate VCO**: primary sound source, typically 20 Hz – 20 kHz range
- **LFO (Low Frequency Oscillator)**: same circuit, but running below audio rate (~0.01 Hz – 20 Hz). Used as a modulation source, not heard directly.
  - LFO → VCO pitch = vibrato
  - LFO → VCF cutoff = filter wobble / wah
  - LFO → VCA = tremolo (amplitude modulation)
  - LFO → PWM = pulse width animation

## 4. VCF — Voltage Controlled Filter

Shapes the timbre of the sound by attenuating certain frequency ranges.

### Filter Types
- **Low-pass filter (LPF)**: passes frequencies below the cutoff, attenuates above. Most common in subtractive synthesis. The "cutoff sweep" sound.
- **High-pass filter (HPF)**: passes frequencies above the cutoff, attenuates below. Thins out the sound.
- **Band-pass filter (BPF)**: passes a band of frequencies around the cutoff. Nasal, focused sound.
- **Notch (band-reject)**: attenuates a narrow band, passes everything else.
- **State variable / multimode**: offers multiple outputs simultaneously.

### Key Parameters
- **Cutoff frequency**: the frequency at which the filter begins to attenuate. CV-controllable.
- **Resonance (Q)**: emphasizes frequencies near the cutoff, creating a ringing peak. At high resonance, many filters self-oscillate — producing a sine wave even with no audio input.
- **Filter slope**: measured in dB per octave (12 dB = 2-pole, 24 dB = 4-pole). Steeper = more dramatic effect.
- **Key tracking**: a portion of the pitch CV is also routed to the filter cutoff, keeping the filter in a musically related ratio to the note being played. 100% tracking = filter cutoff moves exactly one octave per octave of pitch.

### Filter in the Synthesis Chain
The filter envelope (typically a second EG) sweeps the cutoff frequency over time, simulating how acoustic instruments are often brightest at the attack transient and then soften. Classic: fast attack, medium decay, zero sustain on the filter EG = percussive "pluck" shape.

## 5. VCA — Voltage Controlled Amplifier

Controls the amplitude of a signal based on an incoming CV. The VCA is the element that translates an envelope into dynamics — without it, the oscillator drones forever.

### Core Concept
- Has two inputs: **signal in** (audio or CV to be scaled) and **CV in** (controls the gain)
- Output amplitude = signal amplitude × CV level (roughly)
- At 0V CV: silence (or minimum output)
- At max CV: full signal passes through
- VCAs do NOT need to be in the audio path — they can scale any CV signal

### Linear vs. Exponential Response VCAs
- **Linear VCA**: gain scales linearly with CV. Equal CV steps = equal amplitude steps. Mathematically correct, but sounds like amplitude fades very quickly and then is mostly at full volume — not perceptually even.
- **Exponential VCA**: gain scales exponentially with CV. Matches human hearing (which perceives loudness logarithmically). Equal CV steps = equal perceived volume steps. Sounds more natural for musical amplitude control.
- When pairing: exponential-shaped envelope + linear VCA ≈ linear-shaped envelope + exponential VCA in terms of perceived result. Mixing these produces different characters.

### Extended Uses of VCAs
- **Modulation depth control**: a VCA between an LFO and its destination controls how much modulation is applied. An envelope opens the VCA → modulation fades in/out over the note.
- **Amplitude modulation (AM)**: patching an audio-rate oscillator to the CV input of a VCA whose signal input carries another oscillator = ring modulation / AM synthesis, creating sidebands.
- **Velocity simulation**: a VCA in the CV path (before the main audio VCA) scales the envelope amplitude based on a velocity signal.
- **Dynamic effects routing**: control reverb send level, delay feedback, or any other parameter dynamically.

> "You can never have enough VCAs." — universal modular wisdom

## 6. Envelope Generators (EG / ADSR)

An envelope generator produces a shaped CV trajectory in response to a gate signal. It defines how a parameter changes over time during a note.

### Why Envelopes Exist
All sounds — acoustic or synthetic — have envelopes. A piano note rises quickly, decays slightly, sustains while the key is held, then fades when released. A snare drum attacks instantly and decays to nothing. The envelope generator lets you sculpt this behavior for a synthesized sound. When connected to a VCA, it creates dynamics; when connected to a VCF, it shapes timbre over time.

### ADSR — The Four Segments

```
         /\
        /  \
       /    \_________ (sustain level)
      /                \
-----                   -----
  A     D      S          R
```

1. **Attack**: Time from gate-on to peak level. Short attack = sharp, percussive onset. Long attack = slow swell from silence.
2. **Decay**: Time from peak to sustain level. Controls how quickly the initial transient fades to the held level.
3. **Sustain**: The level held while the gate remains high. This is a **level** (voltage), not a time. Can be 0 (fully percussive) to maximum.
4. **Release**: Time from gate-off to zero. Short release = abrupt cutoff. Long release = gradual fade / reverb-like tail.

### Triggering Behavior
- Gate goes high → attack begins
- Attack reaches peak → decay begins immediately
- Decay reaches sustain level → sustain held as long as gate is high
- Gate goes low → release begins (from wherever the envelope currently is)
- **Early gate-off**: behavior varies by module. Many modules jump immediately to release from wherever attack/decay has reached. Some always complete the attack first. Check module documentation.
- **Retrigger**: if a new gate/trigger arrives before the previous envelope has finished, the envelope resets to attack from its current level (not from zero) — this can cause the "clicking" of retriggered transients.

### Envelope Curve Shapes

The curve shape of each segment profoundly affects the character:

- **Exponential curves** (most common in analog): the rate of change slows as the target is approached. Attack is concave-down (quick rise, then eases in); decay/release are concave-up (fast initial fall, then eases out). Natural-sounding because it mirrors RC circuits (capacitor + resistor) and also mirrors how human hearing perceives level changes.
- **Linear curves**: constant rate of change throughout the segment. Sounds more mechanical and "electronic." Often perceived as slower-sounding than exponential even at the same time setting.
- **Logarithmic/reverse-exponential**: fast at the end of the segment rather than the start. Less common, can be used for special effects.

> Exponential envelope + linear VCA ≈ linear envelope + exponential VCA (in perceived result). The combination matters, not just one component.

> Perceptual note: ultra-fast attacks often *sound* slower than moderately fast attacks due to auditory illusions. Don't chase "maximum snappiness" by making attacks ever shorter — a slightly non-zero attack often sounds crisper.

### Beyond ADSR

- **AD (Attack-Decay)**: no sustain, no release. Fires and returns to zero. Triggered by a trigger (not gate duration). Good for percussion.
- **AR (Attack-Release)**: like a gate-following ramp. Simple and useful.
- **ASR (Attack-Sustain-Release)**: no decay phase. Everything sounds organ-like.
- **Multi-stage / function generators**: arbitrary segment counts and shapes. Example: Befaco Rampage, Maths — can loop, act as LFOs, slew limiters, comparators.
- **Looping envelopes**: when the envelope is set to loop (re-trigger itself at the end of release), it becomes an LFO with a shaped waveform.

### Envelope as a General-Purpose CV

An envelope is just a CV with a particular time shape. It can be used to modulate anything:
- VCF cutoff: filter sweep per note
- Oscillator pitch: pitch drop/rise at note start (important for kick drums — fast pitch decay)
- Delay time, reverb amount, wavefolder depth, panning...

## 7. Sequencers

A sequencer generates sequences of CV and/or gate/trigger signals, automating pitch and rhythm over time.

### Types

- **Step sequencer**: a fixed number of steps (commonly 8, 16, 32), each with a pitch CV value and optionally a gate on/off. Steps advance on each incoming clock pulse.
- **Gate/trigger sequencer**: generates only rhythmic patterns (on/off per step), not pitch. Used for percussion patterns.
- **CV sequencer**: generates only pitch/modulation CV, no gates (paired with a separate gate sequencer).
- **Euclidean sequencer**: distributes a set number of triggers evenly across a number of steps using the Euclidean algorithm. Creates rhythmically interesting patterns (e.g., 3 hits in 8 steps = [1,0,0,1,0,0,1,0]).
- **Random / generative**: uses probability, random voltages (S&H), or algorithmic rules to generate evolving sequences.

### Key Concepts

- **Clock input**: each clock pulse advances the sequencer one step. Clock rate = tempo. Clock division (÷2, ÷4) = slower sequence; multiplication (×2) = faster.
- **Reset**: a trigger input that forces the sequencer back to step 1, allowing synchronization.
- **Gate length**: the proportion of each step that the gate is held high. Affects perceived articulation (legato vs. staccato).
- **Quantization**: rounding pitch CV to the nearest semitone (or scale degree). A quantizer module takes arbitrary CV and snaps it to musical pitches. Can be configured for specific scales/modes.
- **Transpose**: shifting all CV steps up or down by a fixed voltage — typically done by adding a CV offset to the sequencer output.

## 8. LFOs — Low Frequency Oscillators

An LFO is simply a VCO running below the audio range, used as a modulation source rather than a sound source. The conceptual distinction is frequency and intent — the circuits are often identical or similar.

### Typical Rate Range
- 0.01 Hz (very slow, ~100 second cycle) to ~20 Hz (boundary of audio range)
- Some LFOs can run into audio rate (becoming oscillators usable for FM/AM)

### Waveforms and Their Uses
- **Sine**: smooth, natural-feeling periodic modulation (vibrato, tremolo)
- **Triangle**: like sine but with linear edges; slightly sharper feel
- **Square**: abrupt transitions — useful for trills, alternating between two states
- **Sawtooth / Ramp**: gradual rise, sudden fall (or reverse). Good for filter "breathing," stepped effects
- **Sample & Hold (S&H)**: random stepped voltages, updated on a trigger. Classic "computer" sound. Often generated by clocking an S&H with an LFO and feeding it white noise.

### LFO Sync
LFOs can be synced to a master clock, locking their rate to a musical division (1 bar, 1/4 note, etc.). This keeps modulation rhythmically related to the sequence tempo.

## 9. Utilities: The Glue of a Modular System

### Attenuator
Scales a signal down (multiplies by a value 0–1). Simple but essential for controlling modulation depth.

### Attenuverter
Scales a signal from -1 to +1, allowing polarity inversion as well as attenuation. A negative value flips the CV direction (e.g., a rising envelope causes the filter to close rather than open).

### Offset / Voltage Source
Adds a fixed DC voltage to a signal, or provides a manual CV source. Used to transpose sequences, bias VCA levels, shift filter cutoff.

### Mixer (CV or Audio)
Sums multiple signals. In the CV domain, mixing two LFOs creates a more complex modulation shape. In audio, mixing multiple oscillators creates richer timbres.

### Multiple / Buffered Multiple
Splits one signal to multiple destinations. An unbuffered (passive) multiple shares voltage directly — acceptable for audio but may cause pitch drift when sharing 1V/oct CV among multiple oscillators. A buffered multiple uses op-amps to drive each output independently, preserving voltage accuracy.

### Sample & Hold (S&H)
On a trigger, "samples" the current value of an input and holds it until the next trigger. Classic use: noise into S&H triggered by a clock = random stepped CV (quantized to pitch = random melody).

### Slew Limiter / Lag Processor
Smooths sudden voltage transitions, adding glide/portamento. Can turn a stepped sequence into a smooth glide. Also acts as a basic function generator (attack = slew up rate, decay = slew down rate). Filters are essentially slew limiters with resonance.

### Logic Modules (AND, OR, XOR)
Combine gate signals. AND = gate only if both inputs are high. OR = gate if either is high. XOR = gate if exactly one is high. Powerful for creating complex rhythmic interactions.

### Clock Divider / Multiplier
Derives slower or faster clock signals from a master clock. ÷2 = half tempo, ÷4 = quarter tempo. Essential for polyrhythm and structured rhythmic relationships.

### Quantizer
Rounds incoming CV to the nearest note of a defined scale/tuning. Can be configured for specific modes, microtonal scales, or custom mappings. Key for turning generative or random CV into musical pitches.

## 11. Patching Philosophy

- **Everything is a voltage**: audio, CV, gates, triggers — they are all voltages. Any output can technically go to any input. Unexpected connections are a primary creative tool.
- **Signal flow direction**: outputs → inputs. Never connect two outputs together without a mixer (may damage modules).
- **Normalization**: some modules have "normalled" connections — a default internal connection that is overridden when a cable is plugged in. Common in mixers and semi-modular instruments.
- **Multiple instances**: the reason "you can never have enough VCAs" is that VCAs are not just for audio amplitude — they are the primary tool for dynamically controlling anything with anything else. Same for attenuators, mixers, and envelope generators.
- **Self-patching**: feeding a module's output back to its own input. Common for oscillator FM (self-FM = wavefolding-like effects), envelope feedback, etc.

## 12. AHD Envelopes

AHD stands for **Attack, Hold, Decay** — a three-stage envelope that omits sustain and release entirely. It is a one-shot (trigger-based) shape that always runs to completion regardless of gate length.

```
         /‾‾‾‾‾\
        /        \
       /          \
------              ------
   A      H      D
```

1. **Attack**: rises from zero to peak (same as ADSR attack)
2. **Hold**: stays at peak level for a defined time before beginning to fall. This is the key distinction from AD envelopes — the hold segment maintains the peak, which is critical for certain sounds where you want a moment of full amplitude before the decay begins.
3. **Decay**: falls from peak to zero. No sustain, no release — the envelope is fully determined by the trigger, not the gate length.

### AHD vs. AD
- An AD envelope goes immediately from peak into decay. There is no dwell time at the top.
- AHD inserts a flat hold period at the peak — useful when you want an audible transient "top" before the tail.
- Both are fundamentally one-shot: they fire completely from a trigger, making them ideal for percussion and other non-sustained sounds.

### AHD vs. ADSR
- ADSR is gate-length dependent (sustain continues as long as gate is high)
- AHD always runs the same shape regardless of gate length
- AHD cannot produce sustained notes; ADSR can
- For drum synthesis and percussive patches, AHD is often preferred because it gives predictable, consistent hit shapes

### Typical Uses
- **Kick drum pitch envelope**: fast attack, zero hold, fast exponential decay drives the pitch down from a high frequency — the classic "thump" of an electronic kick
- **Snare body/noise**: short attack, zero hold, medium decay controls the amplitude of the noise burst
- **Any percussive hit**: where you want the envelope to be self-contained and not affected by how long the sequencer gate is held open
- **Modulation**: AHD as a CV source for filter cutoff, wavefolder, or other parameters on percussion hits

### Hold as a Musical Parameter
The hold segment is underrated. In drum synthesis:
- Short hold + long decay = natural, rounded hit
- Long hold = sustained "gate open" body before the tail (useful for industrial/synthetic sounds, or simulating a held pad hit)
- Zero hold = same as AD envelope

Some modules label this differently: "sustain" on a percussive envelope module may actually function as a hold (a timed flat top), not as a level-based sustain as in ADSR.

## 13. Drum Synthesis

Electronic drum sounds are synthesized by combining a small number of fundamental building blocks — oscillators, noise, envelopes, filters, and VCAs — in specific ways. Understanding these recipes is essential for designing drum-related modules and algorithms.

### Kick Drum

The classic electronic kick (as in TR-808 style) is built from:

1. **Sine or triangle oscillator**: the body of the kick. No harmonics needed — the low-frequency fundamental is the sound.
2. **Pitch envelope (AHD or AD)**: a fast-decay envelope sweeps the oscillator frequency from a high starting pitch (often 150–400 Hz) down to a low resting pitch (40–80 Hz). The speed and curve of this sweep defines the character ("click" transient vs. smooth "thud").
3. **Amplitude envelope (AHD or AD)**: controls the overall volume shape. Typically a fast attack and a longer exponential decay (200–800 ms).
4. **Optional: VCF on the body**: a low-pass filter can roll off the top end of the body oscillator to tune the "thickness."

Key parameters:
- **Pitch start / pitch end**: define the sweep range
- **Pitch decay time**: fast = punchy click; slow = longer tonal "note"
- **Amplitude decay time**: short = tight; long = boomy
- **Body tuning**: the resting pitch of the oscillator

Exponential pitch decay is critical — linear sweeps sound unnatural. The ear perceives pitch logarithmically, so an exponential curve in the pitch CV produces a perceptually even sweep.

### Snare Drum

A snare is typically two layers mixed together:

1. **Tone body**: one or two sine/triangle oscillators at relatively low frequencies (~180–250 Hz). Short AHD envelope — this is the "thwack."
2. **Noise burst**: white or pink noise through a VCA with a short-to-medium AHD envelope. This is the "snare rattle." A high-pass or band-pass filter shapes the noise character.
3. **Mix of both**: the ratio of tone to noise determines whether the snare sounds more "acoustic" (more tone) or "electronic" (more noise).

Optional: a pitch envelope on the tone oscillator (like a mini kick pitch sweep) adds snap to the attack.

### Hi-Hat

Hi-hats are almost entirely noise — specifically filtered noise:

1. **White noise** (or a mix of high-frequency square waves — the original TR-808 technique uses 6 detuned square oscillators)
2. **High-pass or band-pass filter**: removes the low end, leaving only the bright "shhh" character
3. **VCA with envelope**:
   - **Closed hi-hat**: very short AD envelope (a few milliseconds to ~100 ms decay)
   - **Open hi-hat**: same attack, much longer decay (~300 ms – 1 s)
   - Open and closed are often the same circuit with two different decay times, and the closed hat is triggered to cut off the open hat's tail

### Clap

Electronic claps simulate the layered, slightly smeared sound of multiple hands:

1. **Noise burst through VCA**: multiple very short noise bursts in rapid succession (simulating separate hand slaps), followed by a longer noise tail (the "reverb" of the room)
2. **Band-pass filter**: shapes the noise into the characteristic "snap" frequency range (~1–3 kHz)
3. Alternatively: a noise burst with an amplitude envelope that has a fast attack and a "bumpy" decay (some modules simulate this with multiple re-triggers in quick succession)

### Tom / Low Percussion

Similar to kick but at a higher pitch range:

1. **Sine or triangle oscillator** at a higher base frequency (~80–200 Hz)
2. **Pitch envelope**: smaller, faster sweep than a kick
3. **Amplitude envelope**: medium decay

### 808-Style Bass (pitched kick / bass)

When the kick's pitch decay is slowed and the resting pitch is raised into a musical range, the kick becomes a pitched bass tone — the classic "808 bass." The pitch can then be controlled via 1V/oct CV, allowing it to be sequenced musically. The VCA decay determines the note length.

### General Drum Synthesis Principles

- **Pitch envelopes are everything**: the character of a percussive sound is defined more by its pitch trajectory than by its waveform. Even a simple sine oscillator becomes a convincing drum with the right pitch envelope.
- **Exponential decays sound natural**: both amplitude and pitch decays should be exponential (not linear) to match acoustic behavior and human perception.
- **Short is tight, long is loose**: amplitude decay time is the primary control over the "feel" of a hit in a mix.
- **Noise character**: white noise has equal energy per frequency; pink noise has more low-end energy (sounds "warmer"). The filter on a noise source dramatically changes the character.
- **Layering**: most convincing drum sounds use at least two layers (e.g., tone + noise for snare, or click + body for kick). The mix ratio shapes the character.
- **Waveshaping / saturation on kick**: driving the kick oscillator slightly into saturation (clipping or soft folding) adds harmonics and presence, making it cut through a mix better.
